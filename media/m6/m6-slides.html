Toolset twin: queues and maintenance

From one furnace to a line: where lots wait, what a change to one tool does to the whole line, and what maintenance costs.

SemiAcademy · ISEP-CSUCC BY 4.0

By the end of this module you can

  • measure throughput, cycle time and work in progress at the same boundary, and check them with Little's law;
  • explain why queues grow faster than load, and find the bottleneck of a line;
  • build a discrete-event simulation from lots, tools and queues, as in SimPy;
  • choose a warm-up, run replications and report a mean with a confidence interval;
  • compare a what-if change by the mean and spread over replications, on the same random numbers;
  • compare preventive and predictive maintenance using the Module 5 drift estimate, and say what a three-tool model leaves out.

Course outcome 6. The lab runs a supplied SimPy model of a furnace, lithography and etch line.

In a line, a lot's time is mostly waiting

  • The lab's line: a lot needs 4.0 hours of processing in all, and spends 7.6 hours in the line.
  • The other 3.6 hours are spent in queues, and 2.3 of them in front of lithography.
  • A twin of one tool cannot see this: the wait depends on the other lots, the tools they use and how many tools there are.
  • Simulated line, mean of 20 replications after the warm-up.

Little's law

L = λ · W
  • The staircase is the number of lots in the line. Its area can be counted two ways: slice by slice of time, which gives L times the window, or lot by lot, which gives the sum of the hours each lot spends in the line.
  • Little (1961) proved it for any arrival pattern and any service times. It needs only the same boundary and a steady state.
  • Lab line, hours 870 to 910 of one replication: an area of 309 lot-hours over 40 hours is 7.7 lots; adding up the hours of each lot inside the window gives the same 309.

Pause and predict

Little's law

The line releases 0.85 lots per hour and lots leave at 0.836 lots per hour. A lot spends 7.56 hours in the line on average, and 3.27 hours in the lithography group, waiting and processing together.

Predict: how many lots are in the line on average, and how many are in the lithography group? Then: what would you get by multiplying 0.836 by lithography's 3.27 hours and comparing it with the lots in the whole line?

Whole line: L = λ · W = 0.836 × 7.56 = 6.3 lots; the simulation measures 6.3. Lithography group: 0.836 × 3.27 = 2.7 lots, and the simulation measures 2.7. Mixing the two, 2.7 against the line's 6.3 lots, gives numbers that do not match: the law has not failed, it was applied to two different systems. L, λ and W must describe the same boundary.

Waiting grows faster than load

  • For one tool, the mean wait in the queue is about (variability) × (utilization) × (process time): Wq ≈ (c_a² + c_s²)/2 · ρ/(1 − ρ) · t_e (Kingman, 1961; Hopp and Spearman, 2011).
  • The factor ρ/(1 − ρ) is 1 at 50 % utilization, 4 at 80 %, 9 at 90 %, 19 at 95 %.
  • In the simulated line the cycle time rises from 4.7 h at a load of 0.5 to 7.6 h at 0.84 and 11.2 h at 0.92, and the spread between replications grows with it.
  • Small changes at a busy tool matter much more than at an idle one.

Discrete-event simulation with SimPy

  • The state changes only at events: a lot arrives, a tool becomes free, a process ends.
  • The simulation keeps a list of future events and jumps to the next one: hours of waiting cost no computing time.
  • SimPy writes each lot as a process (a Python generator) and each tool group as a resource with a queue.
  • Random numbers make every run different: each run is a replication, started from a seed.
def lot(i):
    tube = yield tubes.get()
    yield env.timeout(furnace_time[i])
    yield tubes.put(tube)

    with litho.request() as req:
        yield req            # wait here
        yield env.timeout(litho_time[i])

    with etch.request() as req:
        yield req
        yield env.timeout(etch_time[i])

Simplified from the lab's line.py; SimPy 4.1.2, MIT license.

One replication

  • The line starts empty, fills within a day, and then moves around seven lots. The average after the warm-up is 7.8 lots in this replication, 6.3 over the 20.
  • One replication is one random run of the line. Everything measured from it, a mean cycle time or an average WIP, has a spread that only several replications can show.
Module 6 · Toolset twin: queues and maintenance10

Warm-up: when does the line settle?

  • The first lots meet no queue, so the first hours are not typical of the running line.
  • Welch's method (1983): average the replications bin by bin, smooth with a moving average, and read where the curve levels off.
  • Here the curve rises from 4 lots in the first 10 hours and levels near 7 after about 120 hours. The lab discards the first 200 hours.
  • The bias of keeping the warm-up is small beside the noise of 20 replications, and discarding it costs a fifth of the run: a cheap safeguard.
Module 6 · Toolset twin: queues and maintenance11

Replications and the confidence interval

  • The 20 replications give mean cycle times from 6.1 to 9.1 hours: one run says little.
  • They are independent runs of one line, so their mean estimates the line's long-run value, and mean ± t · s / √n is a 95 % confidence interval.
  • With n = 20, t = 2.09: mean 7.56 h, half-width 0.41 h.
  • The half-width shrinks about as 1/√n: 0.96 h with 5 replications, 0.63 with 10, 0.41 with 20.

Pause and predict

How many replications?

With 20 replications the half-width of the interval for the mean cycle time is 0.41 hours.

Predict: about how many replications would it take to bring the half-width down to 0.2 hours?

The half-width goes as 1/√n, so halving it takes four times as many replications. From 0.41 to 0.2 is a factor of 2.07, which takes about 2.07² × 20 = 86 replications (a few fewer, because t also falls as n grows). Precision is expensive: ten times the precision costs a hundred times the runs.

Where the time goes: the bottleneck

  • Lithography has the highest utilization, 0.84, and the longest wait, 2.3 h.
  • The furnace, at 0.75 with two tubes, waits 1.3 h. Etch, at 0.50, almost never makes a lot wait.
  • The bottleneck is where a change to a process time buys the most cycle time; elsewhere it buys little.
  • Mean of 20 replications, after the warm-up.

Pause and predict

Which 10 % is worth more?

Two changes, each shortening a process time by 10 %: the etch time from 1.2 to 1.08 hours, or the lithography time from 1.0 to 0.9 hours. The mean cycle time is 7.6 hours.

Predict: which change shortens the cycle time more, and by roughly how much?

Lithography, by 1.35 h (interval ± 0.22). Etch saves 0.14 h, about what its shorter process time removes (0.12 h). Lithography is the bottleneck: speeding it shortens its queue as well as its process time.

Same random numbers, paired differences

  • Between seeds, the baseline's cycle time ranges over about 3 hours. The etch change saves 0.13 to 0.15 hours.
  • Replications with the same seed use the same lots, arrival times and unit process times in both lines. Each seed gives one difference, and the 20 differences go through the same interval formula.
  • Paired: 0.139 ± 0.002 h, positive in all 20. Unpaired, the two intervals (± 0.41 h each) overlap and cannot tell.
  • Say which you did: paired differences are sharper, and only valid when both lines see the same random numbers.

What a three-tool model leaves out

Re-entrant flow

A lot returns to lithography and etch for every layer, dozens of times. The bottleneck depends on the layer mix, and lots of different ages compete for the same tools.

Many products and priorities

Different recipes, set-ups between them, rush lots that jump the queue.

Batching rules

A real furnace processes many lots at once, and the rule for filling a batch changes the queues.

Other things that go wrong

Random breakdowns, operators, transport, rework loops. The model has drift and calibration only.

Each can move the bottleneck. The model answers the questions it was built for, such as how queues respond to load and to one change; it needs data from a real line before it is trusted for more.

Summary

  • Throughput, cycle time and work in progress are tied by L = λW, when all three describe the same boundary.
  • Queues grow faster than load: the busiest tool, the bottleneck, decides where a change matters.
  • A discrete-event simulation gives each statistic as a mean with an interval, from replications that start after a warm-up.
  • Compare what-if changes on the same random numbers, and report the paired difference with its interval.
  • The Module 5 drift estimate turns maintenance from a calendar into a decision for each tube, and the twin shows what that costs.
