Keeping the twin in sync

Estimate how far the furnace has drifted from its twin, correct the next run, and know when to call for a calibration.

SemiAcademy · ISEP-CSUCC BY 4.0

By the end of this module you can

  • explain how a growing thermocouple bias thins the oxide while the furnace's own data look normal;
  • define the offset d between the furnace and its twin, and estimate it with an EWMA and a one-variable Kalman filter;
  • set the next oxidation time with the inverted Deal-Grove model, within the recipe limits;
  • compare controllers on the same disturbances by their mean absolute error, largest deviation and runs held at a limit;
  • tell advisory from automatic mode, and decide when to recalibrate or call maintenance.

Course outcome 5. The update equations are given, not derived; the lab runs every controller on your own furnace.

Module 5 · Keeping the twin in sync2

Why the twin falls out of sync

  • A thermocouple's calibration changes with use at high temperature: its wires are contaminated and damaged, slowly and by an unknown amount (Machin, Tucker and Pearce, 2021).
  • Here it reads high by a bias b. The controller holds the reading at 1000 °C, so the wafers see 1000 °C − b.
  • Every trace says 1000 °C: the furnace's own data cannot show the drift.
  • Only a measurement of the product, the oxide thickness, can.

The state the twin estimates: the offset d

  • d = measured monitor-wafer thickness − the twin's prediction for that run's recipe.
  • At qualification, d_ref = 6.45 nm: Deal-Grove leaves out the fast early growth on bare wafers (Module 3). A known, steady offset is no problem.
  • As the bias grows, d falls: that change is the drift.
  • One monitor wafer per run is the only observation, and some runs have none.

  • The run-to-run noise, about 0.4 nm in one observation, hides the trend from run to run; the drift wins over many runs.

The run-to-run loop

  • Run-to-run control combines statistical process control with feedback between runs (Sachs, Hu and Ingolfsson, 1995); it is a standard part of fab process control.
  • In a fab it runs in the factory's process control systems; SEMI E133 standardizes how such systems, run-to-run control and fault detection among them, share data.

EWMA: a weighted memory of the offset

d̂_new = λ · y + (1 − λ) · d̂_old    (no measurement: d̂_new = d̂_old)
  • y is the offset observed in the newest run: measured − predicted.
  • Unrolled, the estimate weights the observations λ, λ(1 − λ), λ(1 − λ)², ... going back.
  • Large λ: follows changes fast and passes on noise. Small λ: smooth, but lags a drift.
  • λ = 1 trusts only the last run.

Pause and predict

One EWMA update

The estimate is d̂ = 4.90 nm. The next run's monitor wafer gives an offset y = 4.50 nm. λ = 0.2.

Predict: the new estimate. Then: what if that run had no measurement?

d̂ = 0.2 × 4.50 + 0.8 × 4.90 = 0.90 + 3.92 = 4.82 nm: one fifth of the way from 4.90 toward 4.50. With no measurement there is nothing to blend in, and the estimate stays at 4.90 nm.

The recipe rule: invert Deal-Grove

x = target − d̂     t = (x² − xᵢ² + A·(x − xᵢ)) / B,   A = B / (B/A)
  • The furnace grows the twin's prediction plus d, so the twin aims at the target minus d̂.
  • From x² + A·x = B·(t + τ), with τ = (xᵢ² + A·xᵢ)/B: the same Deal-Grove model as Module 3, solved for t.
  • Clip t to the recipe limits, 80 to 100 minutes. A clipped run is held at a limit: it cannot reach the target.
  • Example furnace: B = 177.21 nm²/min, B/A = 0.7329 nm/min, target 61.2 nm. With d̂ = d_ref = 6.45 nm: t = 89.56 min.

Pause and predict

Set the next run

Same furnace: B = 177.21 nm²/min, B/A = 0.7329 nm/min, xᵢ = 1.5 nm, target 61.2 nm, limits 80 to 100 minutes. After some drift, d̂ = 4.20 nm.

Predict: the oxidation time. Longer or shorter than 89.56 minutes?

x = 61.2 − 4.20 = 57.00 nm; A = 177.21 / 0.7329 = 241.8 nm; t = (57.00² − 1.5² + 241.8 × 55.50) / 177.21 = 94.05 min. Longer: the furnace now grows less, so the twin must aim higher. It is inside the limits.

A one-variable Kalman filter

model: d_k = d_(k−1) + w (variance Q)    y_k = d_k + v (variance R)
P⁻ = P + Q    K = P⁻ / (P⁻ + R)    d̂ = d̂ + K · (y − d̂)    P = (1 − K) · P⁻
  • P is the variance of the estimate. Q: how much the offset may move per run. R: the noise of one observation.
  • R = 0.16 nm² from the qualification study (temperature noise and the monitor wafer's measurement); Q = 0.01 nm², a little larger than the random part of the drift.
  • No measurement: only P⁻ = P + Q. The estimate stays; its uncertainty grows.
  • Kalman (1960); for one variable these four lines are the whole filter.
Module 5 · Keeping the twin in sync13

The gain settles, and an EWMA appears

  • From P = R, the gain starts at 0.52: the filter learns fast at first.
  • It settles at 0.22 within about ten runs: from then on the filter is an EWMA with λ = 0.22.
  • Muth (1960): exponential smoothing is the best forecast for a random walk observed with noise.
  • After a missing run, P has grown, so the next measurement gets more weight.

Pause and predict

One Kalman update

d̂ = 4.90 nm with P = 0.035 nm². The next observation is y = 4.50 nm. Q = 0.01 nm², R = 0.16 nm².

Predict: the gain, the new estimate and its variance.

P⁻ = 0.035 + 0.01 = 0.045; K = 0.045 / (0.045 + 0.16) = 0.220; d̂ = 4.90 + 0.220 × (4.50 − 4.90) = 4.812 nm; P = (1 − 0.220) × 0.045 = 0.0351 nm². The gain is close to the EWMA's 0.2, and so is the result.

Choosing λ: noise against lag

  • Small λ averages over many runs and trails the drift: λ = 0.05 gives 0.74 nm.
  • λ = 1 copies each run's noise into the next recipe: 0.38 nm.
  • In between, near λ = 0.3, the error is lowest: 0.24 nm.
  • The best λ depends on how fast the offset moves compared with the noise: what Q and R state.
Module 5 · Keeping the twin in sync17

Gaps, advisory mode and automatic mode

  • No measurement (run 39 here): both estimators keep d̂, so the next recipe is unchanged; the Kalman filter also widens P. A twin should report the extra uncertainty.
  • Advisory mode: the controller recommends and an engineer accepts each change. Reviewed once every three runs, the Kalman controller's error rises only from 0.250 to 0.263 nm here, because the drift is slow.
  • Automatic mode: every recommendation runs at once. No delay and no workload, but nobody looks at each decision: the controller needs limits and alarms.
  • A new controller usually starts in advisory mode, until its recommendations have earned trust.

When to recalibrate, when to call maintenance

Recalibrate when |d̂ − d_ref| > 2.0 nm for 3 runs in a row: the drift is about 4 °C. In the drift scenario the alarm fires at run 46 while the thickness is still on target. A failing thermocouple (above) drives the time to its limit: held for 9 runs, off target by up to 3.5 nm. The alarm came 10 runs earlier: stop and call maintenance.

Summary

  • A growing thermocouple bias thins the oxide while every trace looks normal; only the measured thickness shows it.
  • The twin tracks one number, the offset d, with an EWMA or a Kalman filter.
  • The recipe rule inverts Deal-Grove to aim at the target minus d̂, within the recipe limits.
  • Compare controllers on the same disturbances: mean error, largest deviation, runs held.
  • A controller hides the drift it corrects: watch d̂, recalibrate in time, call maintenance when the recipe runs out of range.
